# Drafting method: linkage first, detail second

A step-by-step method for building an application from the Specific Aims page outward. It is adapted, in this skill's own words, from the approach taught by Russell and Morrison in *The Grant Application Writer's Workbook* (Grant Writers' Seminars and Workshops), which the investigator supplied as a working reference. The book itself is copyrighted and is not part of this skill or the repository; do not quote passages from it. Where its general-agency advice differs from current NIH practice, the NIH adaptation is given and wins.

Use this file when an investigator is starting from a blank page, when an aims page "doesn't hang together", or when coaching someone through a full draft. `nih-writing-craft.md` covers what each section must achieve; this file covers the order and mechanics of getting there.

## 1. Principles

- **The funder is buying progress on its mission, not supporting the applicant's interests.** Every section should make it easy to see how the work advances the mission of NIH and of the specific institute. Read the institute's strategic plan and say, more than once, how the project serves it.
- **Vet the idea before writing.** Have the investigator write the idea as one short paragraph, list its strengths and weaknesses and how each weakness could be fixed (collaborator, consultant, resource), check the literature and funded-awards databases (NIH RePORTER) for competitors, and talk it through with at least three colleagues. Add a program officer to that list.
- **Write for the reviewer you will actually get.** Assume someone intelligent but outside the exact subfield, tired, reading under time pressure, and reading because they must. Assigned reviewers need enough depth to defend the application; everyone else on the panel votes after reading little more than the aims page.
- **Lead reviewers to advocacy.** The aim is a reader who finishes page one wanting this funded and able to explain why to the panel.
- **Concept before detail, everywhere.** Each section, aim and paragraph opens with what matters and why, then gives specifics. Detail that arrives before the reader cares is noise.
- **Time is the limiting resource.** The method assumes months, not weeks: roughly two protected hours a day over about four months for a full research application. When an investigator arrives three weeks before the deadline, say what can realistically be done well and what the trade-offs are.

## 2. Order of work

Write in the order that makes each part easy, not the order of the form:

1. Aims page (the master plan). Do not go on until it survives outside readers.
2. Approach, aim by aim, as an expansion of the aims page.
3. Significance and Innovation, now that the claims they must support are fixed.
4. Preliminary data placement and the background needed to support the hypothesis.
5. Biosketches, facilities, letters, budget (the budget cannot be right until the Approach is).
6. Title, Abstract and Project Narrative last, when you know what the application says.

## 3. The aims page as twelve linked elements

Four paragraphs. Help the investigator fill each element with one bullet first.

**Paragraph 1: why this matters and what is missing**
1. *Opening.* A sentence that tells any reader what area this is and why NIH would care. Do not open with "The purpose of this application is"; the reader does not yet know why the purpose matters.
2. *Key knowns.* Three or four sentences that funnel from the field to the exact spot the project addresses. Only what the reader needs in order to see the gap; not a review.
3. *The gap or unmet need, and why its persistence is a problem.* The engine of the whole application. Stated plainly, it should feel like the obvious next step from the knowns.

By the end of this paragraph the reader knows there is a problem that needs solving and needs no further background to follow the rest of the page.

**Paragraph 2: what will be done, and by whom**
4. *Long-term goal.* The investigator's research program over many years, broad enough to hold several grants, visibly aligned with the institute's mission, and not overstated.
5. *Objective of this application.* The defined end point for this award. If the linkage is right, the objective is to fill the gap named in element 3, and it is a step toward element 4.
6. *Central hypothesis* (or, for work that tests nothing, a statement of need), followed immediately by how it was formed, usually from the team's preliminary data plus the literature. A real hypothesis is directional, objectively testable, and has a conceivable alternative outcome; if no reasonable person could imagine it being wrong, it is a truism. It is what prevents the "fishing expedition" critique.
7. *Rationale.* What becomes possible once this work is done that is not possible now. Not "why the topic is interesting".
8. *Why this team and place.* Only the two or three facts that give this group an edge: the hard method already working in their hands, unique access to a population or data resource, the specific collaborator. For UNM applicants, this is where one concrete institutional asset from `unm-data/` belongs.

Keep elements 4 through 6 adjacent, with no explanatory material wedged between them.

**Paragraph 3: the aims**
9. *Two to four aims.* Each a short conceptual headline that conveys why the work is being done, not a description of procedures, followed by its working hypothesis. Related to each other, but none dependent on a particular result of another.

**Paragraph 4: the payoff**
10. *Innovation,* only if a credible, specific case exists.
11. *Expected outcomes,* about one per aim: the deliverables NIH gets for its investment. Together they should confirm or refute the central hypothesis and so achieve the objective.
12. *Impact.* One or two general sentences on what applying the results will do for the field and for health. This hands off to the Significance section.

Keep citations and methodological detail off this page; both dilute it and both are supplied later.

## 4. The linkage test

Most weak aims pages contain all the right parts with broken connections. Check each link in turn and fix the first one that fails:

| Link | Question |
|---|---|
| Knowns to gap | Does the gap follow directly from what was just said is known? |
| Gap to objective | Is the objective, almost word for word, "close that gap"? |
| Objective to long-term goal | Is the objective clearly one step toward the goal? |
| Objective to hypothesis | Would testing the hypothesis achieve the objective? |
| Hypothesis to aims | Do the aims, taken together, fully test the central hypothesis, each through its own working hypothesis? |
| Aims to expected outcomes | Is there a concrete outcome for each aim? |
| Outcomes to gap | Would those outcomes, collectively, fill the gap in paragraph 1? |
| Impact to mission | Does the impact statement connect to what the institute exists to do? |

If a reader has to stop and work out how one element relates to the next, the link is broken. The same chain should be traceable in the Abstract and at the top of each aim in the Approach, using the same wording for the same thing.

## 5. Bullets before sentences, colleagues before chapters

- Draft each of the twelve elements as a single bullet. With no prose in the way, gaps in logic and stray detail are easy to see. Only when the bullets link do they become sentences, then the four paragraphs.
- This is also the natural way to work with an investigator under NIH's originality policy: they supply the bullets, the skill tests the links and helps turn their bullets into clear sentences.
- Then get the page read by at least three people, some in the field and some outside it, with a turnaround of two or three days. Give most weight to comments about not understanding something. Revise and send it back to the harshest reader plus someone new. Continue until readers agree it is clear. Offer to play the outside reader, but say that real colleagues and the program officer matter more.

## 6. Significance: one explicit sentence, then benefits

Build the argument in three moves:
1. *Substantiate the need,* with primary citations and authoritative statistics, expanding (not repeating) the opening of the aims page.
2. *State the significance in one explicit sentence,* set off so a reviewer hunting for it finds it: this work is significant because it will do X. Reviewers write a significance paragraph in every critique; give them the sentence.
3. *List the benefits that follow* once the results are applied, including those others will realize by building on the work, and any spill-over to neighboring fields. Benefits must be specific and credible, not slogans.

NIH adaptation: Significance is a section of the Research Strategy that must also address the rigor of the prior research (see `nih-writing-craft.md`). Keep the explicit sentence and benefits list, and add the critical assessment of prior work.

## 7. Approach: the same four-part unit for every aim

Repeat each aim's title exactly as it appears on the aims page, then:

- **Introduction** (about a quarter to a third of a page): a mini-overview. One or two orienting facts; why this aim is needed; the aim's objective, defined by an end point, not a method; the working hypothesis, repeated essentially verbatim from the aims page; the approach in broad strokes; the rationale (what becomes possible after this aim); and the overall expected result and why it matters.
- **Studies:** one conceptual unit per paragraph, each under an informative title that says what is being learned, not a generic label such as "Data collection". For each, outline before writing: why it is needed, design, methods, key reagents or instruments, participants or animals and how the number was derived, power or statistics, controls, what result is expected and how it will be interpreted, time needed. Detail only methods that are new to the team or unfamiliar to reviewers; less-established investigators need to show more. No later study should depend on one particular outcome of an earlier one.
- **Expected outcomes** (a third to half a page): gather the expected results of the studies and say how, together, they achieve the aim's objective and feed the overall objective. Confident, with honest caveats.
- **Potential problems and alternative strategies** (a third to half a page): begin with what happens if the working hypothesis is wrong. Then the few most plausible problems, each with why it is unlikely and what would be done instead. These should be surmountable problems; a problem that could sink the aim belongs in the design, not here.

Give aims roughly equal space unless their difficulty differs. Close the Approach with a **timeline** showing each aim and its main studies across the project years, and a short **future directions** paragraph that places the completed work on the path to the long-term goal. The latter matters most for early-stage investigators, since reviewers are judging whether this person is a good long-term investment.

A general methods subsection is acceptable at the end of the Approach, or at the start when a method (for example, an intervention) is the heart of the design. Never move methods to an appendix or make reviewers flip between sections.

## 8. Preliminary studies and qualifications

- List candidate preliminary results, rank them by how directly they support feasibility or the hypothesis, and write one line on what each proves for this application. Drop the rest.
- Present each as question, method, result, then what it means for the proposed work. Specific findings, not summaries of activity.
- Team and environment: for every collaborator and consultant, write what they contribute and why the project needs them, and draft a letter that names that contribution. For environment, bullet the reasons this setting suits this project before writing any prose.

NIH adaptation: preliminary data go inside the Approach, next to the aim they support, not in a separate section.

## 9. Final checks and pre-submission review

- Read the complete draft once for flow, then put it away for several days and read it again.
- Give the full application to three critical readers with one to two weeks to review, then meet to discuss strengths and weaknesses. This has to be scheduled before the internal routing deadline, which is why the timeline in `nih-application-anatomy.md` starts early.
- Format for the tired reader: space between paragraphs, headings that match the review criteria, strong verbs ("we expect", "we will") over hedges, few abbreviations (keep a list while drafting), nothing that requires turning to another section.

## 10. Where the book's advice needs adapting for NIH today

The method is sound; some specifics predate current NIH rules or were written for funders in general.

| Book's general advice | NIH today |
|---|---|
| Overview of one to one and a half pages | Specific Aims is exactly one page |
| A separate Significance paragraph placed right after the overview | Significance is a Research Strategy subsection and must address rigor of prior research |
| Separate Background and Preliminary Studies sections | Background is folded into Significance; preliminary data sit in the Approach |
| Innovation optional, inside the payoff paragraph | Innovation is its own subsection and part of the Importance of the Research score; still claim only what is real |
| Cite as first author and year for recognizability | Fine if space allows; numbered citations are common under tight page limits. Either way every citation must be verified |
| Suggest reviewers in a cover letter | NIH does not accept reviewer suggestions; use the assignment request form to suggest a study section or expertise, and to name conflicts |
| Italic and bold emphasis for key elements | Use sparingly and stay within NIH font and formatting rules |
| Generous margins | Half-inch minimum; use white space between paragraphs instead |
| Inflation escalators in out-years | Follow current NIH and UNM budgeting practice |
| Reviewer orientation toward investigators and environment as scored criteria | Under the simplified framework, Importance and Rigor and Feasibility are scored; Expertise and Resources is sufficient or insufficient |
| Apply to several agencies with the same idea | Still good strategy, but overlapping applications must be disclosed and cannot both be funded for the same work; NIH also caps applications per PI per year (see `nih-policy-watch.md`) |
