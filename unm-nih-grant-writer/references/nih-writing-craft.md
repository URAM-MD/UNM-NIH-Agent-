# NIH writing craft

How to help an investigator write sections that score. Use this to structure, question and edit the investigator's own material.

## 1. Specific Aims

Many reviewers form their score here; unassigned reviewers may read only this page. Four moves, one page:

1. **Opening paragraph: the problem and the gap.** What is known, what is not, and why the gap matters for health. End on the specific unknown this project addresses, not on the disease burden.
2. **The "what and why us" paragraph.** Long-term goal; objective of this application; central hypothesis and the preliminary evidence it rests on; rationale (what becomes possible once it is done). One clause on why this team and setting are positioned to do it.
3. **The aims.** Two or three. Each has a title that states an outcome, a testable hypothesis or a clear objective, and one or two lines on approach. Each is achievable independently: if Aim 1 fails, Aims 2 and 3 still stand.
4. **Payoff paragraph.** Expected outcomes and what changes in the field or in practice.

Questions to ask the investigator when the page is weak:
- What is the single sentence you want a reviewer to repeat at the study section table?
- What would you conclude if the hypothesis is wrong? If nothing, the aim is descriptive.
- Which result in your preliminary data most makes you believe this?
- What would the next R01 be? That tells you the long-term goal.

Faults to look for: aims that are a list of activities ("to characterize", "to explore") with no hypothesis; a domino structure; four or more aims; a gap statement so broad nothing could close it; innovation claimed but not specified; first paragraph spent on epidemiology the reviewers know.

## 2. Research Strategy

**Significance** answers "if this works completely, so what?" Structure: the important problem; the rigor of the prior research supporting the aims, including weaknesses in that prior work that this project addresses; how the field changes on success. Critiquing the existing literature's rigor is expected, not impolite.

**Innovation** is short and specific. State what is new (concept, method, population, data resource, intervention) against the current standard, and why the new thing is better, not just different. Do not claim innovation for routine use of a new technology.

**Approach** carries the Rigor and Feasibility score. For each aim:
- Rationale and hypothesis, linked to preliminary data shown at that point. Figures must be legible at printed size, with n, statistics and a caption that states the conclusion.
- Design: participants or model, groups, variables, procedures, in enough detail to judge feasibility but not a protocol.
- Analysis: statistical approach per hypothesis, sample size and power with the effect size and where it came from, handling of missing data and multiplicity.
- Expected outcomes and interpretation.
- Potential problems and alternative strategies that are real. Name the likeliest failure and what the team will do; "we do not anticipate problems" reads as inexperience.
- Timeline and, where relevant, milestones.

Give space in proportion to risk: the hardest, most novel aim gets the most detail.

## 3. Rigor

Reviewers are instructed to assess it. The application should make each of these easy to find:
- Rigor of prior research (in Significance).
- Experimental design controls for bias: randomization, blinding, inclusion and exclusion criteria, pre-specified endpoints, replication.
- Relevant biological variables, especially sex, and also age, weight and underlying conditions, considered in design and analysis, or a justified reason for a single-sex study.
- Authentication of key biological and chemical resources (separate attachment).
- A named statistician with real effort when the design calls for one.

## 4. Human subjects, inclusion and recruitment feasibility

- Decide early whether the study meets NIH's clinical trial definition; it changes which NOFOs are eligible and adds required sections.
- Inclusion plans cover sex or gender, race and ethnicity, and age across the lifespan, with scientific justification for any exclusion. The planned enrollment table should be defensible from actual clinic or population numbers.
- Recruitment feasibility is where UNM-specific data wins or loses credibility. Use real counts from `unm-data/` (patients with the condition seen per year at the relevant clinic, prior study accrual, retention) and state the fraction expected to be eligible and to consent. If the library lacks the number, ask the investigator to obtain it from the clinic or the clinical data warehouse instead of estimating.
- Research with tribal communities requires tribal approval processes and often data-sovereignty agreements in addition to the UNM IRB. Put these in the timeline and letters; never imply access that has not been agreed.
- Single IRB applies to multi-site non-exempt studies.

## 5. Facilities & Resources and institutional sections

Purpose: convince a reviewer the environment is sufficient for this project, so Factor 3 is not in doubt.

- Organize by what the project needs: laboratory, clinical, animal, computing, office, and the specific cores used. For each, say which aim uses it and what it enables.
- Include intellectual environment: collaborators nearby, seminars, relevant centers, and for early-stage investigators the institutional investment (start-up, protected time, mentoring, space).
- Cut anything the project does not touch. A two-page targeted section beats ten pages of pasted boilerplate.
- Every number comes from `unm-data/` with status respected. Boilerplate in `unm-data/boilerplate/` is a starting point to tailor, and its date matters: check that centers, directors and equipment are still current.

For K awards, the Institutional Commitment letter must state protected effort percentage, salary support, space, start-up and that the commitment does not depend on the award. Draft it for the chair's signature from facts the chair has confirmed.

## 6. Budget justification

- Modular budgets (R01 up to $250k direct per year, in $25k modules) need only personnel, consortium and any variation justification. Detailed budgets justify every category.
- Personnel: name, role, effort in person-months, what they do tied to aims. Effort must be believable against the work described and against other commitments.
- Apply the NIH salary cap for the current year, UNM fringe rates and the correct F&A rate and base from `unm-data/institution/proposal-logistics.md`. Indirect cost policy has been unstable; see the policy watch.
- Subawards: each needs its own budget, justification and institutional sign-off. Start early.
- Reviewers do not score the budget but will comment on one that does not match the science.

## 7. Career development and fellowship applications

Reviewers fund a person's trajectory. The application must show a gap between where the candidate is and independence, and a plan that closes it.
- Candidate section: a coherent story of prior training leading to this question, evidence of productivity, and a specific statement of what skills are missing.
- Career development plan: each new skill mapped to a mentor, a course or activity, and a time in the award period. A table helps.
- Mentoring team: a primary mentor with funding and a training record, co-mentors covering each skill gap, meeting frequency stated. Mentor statements should be specific to this candidate.
- Research plan: scaled to the award, clearly the candidate's own, and a platform for a named future R01. It should need the training requested.
- Institutional sections: see part 5. For UNM, draw on mentoring programs, CTSC training resources and IDeA infrastructure listed in the library.

## 8. Letters of support

- Collaborators and consultants: state the specific contribution, effort or resources committed, and enthusiasm in one page. Offer to draft for the signer when asked, from facts the signer has agreed to.
- Institutional letters: concrete commitments with numbers.
- Community partners: describe the actual relationship and what the partner will do; identical template letters from many partners weaken an application.
- Letters cannot be used to add preliminary data or evade page limits.

## 9. Resubmissions

- Start from the summary statement. Sort every criticism: fixable by clarification, fixable by new data, fixable by redesign, or disagreement.
- The one-page Introduction thanks reviewers briefly, then answers the major points in order of importance, grouped by theme, stating what changed and where. Concede what was right. For a real disagreement, give evidence and a compromise where possible.
- Respond to the resume and summary of discussion first; it reflects what the panel agreed on.
- Changes are no longer required to be marked in the text; follow the current guide.
- Ask the investigator to call the program officer, who heard the discussion.
- If the score was far from fundable and the critiques were about importance, consider whether a new application with a reframed question serves better than an A1.
- Interim review procedures for 2026 councils changed what "not discussed" means; see the policy watch.

## 10. Common critiques and what prevents them

| Critique | Prevention |
|---|---|
| Overly ambitious | Fewer aims, realistic timeline, show throughput from preliminary work |
| Descriptive, not hypothesis-driven | Each aim tests something with interpretable alternative outcomes |
| Aims interdependent | Restructure so each stands alone; give alternatives |
| Preliminary data do not support feasibility | Show the hardest method working in the team's hands |
| Insufficient statistical detail or power | Named statistician; power per primary hypothesis with justified effect size |
| Lack of rigor, SABV not addressed | Explicit paragraph per aim |
| Recruitment not feasible | Real local numbers, prior accrual, retention plan |
| Investigator lacks expertise in X | Add a collaborator with effort and a letter |
| Environment not well described | Targeted Facilities section tied to aims |
| Incremental | Significance states what changes in the field, not only what is learned |

## 11. Style

- One idea per paragraph, the point in the first sentence. Reviewers skim.
- Headings and a few bold topic phrases help navigation; dense unbroken pages hurt.
- Define every abbreviation and use few.
- Active voice with "we". Confident, not inflated: avoid "novel", "paradigm-shifting", "critical" unless shown.
- Figures near the text that cites them, readable at 100 percent.
- White space is worth the words it costs.
- Keep terminology identical between the aims page, abstract and Research Strategy; renamed aims confuse reviewers.
